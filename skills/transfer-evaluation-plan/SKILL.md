# Transfer Evaluation Plan

Sets out what you'll measure to show a programme changed what people do at work: each measure with its LTEM tier, its baseline, its source, who collects it and when. Or checks a plan someone has already written. Agreed before the course runs, while baselines can still be taken, it gives the exec team evidence of what changed at work and which results it moved.

## When to use

- "How should we evaluate the new management programme?"
- "Our sponsor wants to know if the systems training worked. What should we measure, and when?"
- "Map our evaluation to Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4."
- "We only ever report completions for the compliance refresher. Help me plan something better."
- "Check this evaluation plan before I take it to the Director of People."

Wrong tool for writing the survey itself (use Application Survey), running interviews about why it worked (Success Case Interviews) or reporting the results (Learning Impact Report). If the behaviours and business goals aren't agreed yet, start with Learning Impact Map.

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** the programme: what it covers, who it's for (roles and numbers), how it's delivered and when it runs.
2. **Essential:** the two to five behaviours it should change at work, and the result the sponsor wants to move. [taken from your Learning Impact Map or On-the-Job Objectives; otherwise I'll propose them for you to confirm]
3. Data you already collect and who holds it: LMS records, system data, quality checks, audits, KPIs. [LMS completions and the end-of-course feedback form only]
4. How your leaders talk about evaluation. [LTEM tiers, with Kirkpatrick levels alongside]
5. When and to whom you'll report. [the sponsor and the Director of People at 90 days, with a results update at six months]
6. The smallest group you'll report separately. [10 people]

Never ask for named learners' scores, health information or protected characteristics. This plan works with groups. If the user pastes individual results, set them aside, work from totals and say so.

## Steps

Numbers in square brackets in these steps and the Quality checks are defaults. Use the user's own figure if they give one; otherwise use the default without asking.

1. Read what the user has given, including attachments. If they've given an existing evaluation plan, this is a check job: run the Quality checks on it, list each problem with where it sits and why it matters, then write a corrected plan.
2. Ask for missing essentials in one message, five questions at most, with the defaults shown. If the user says "just draft it", use the defaults and list them as assumptions.
3. Plan from the result down. Write the chain for this programme: the result the sponsor wants (LTEM tier 8), the behaviours at work that should drive it (tier 7), and the decisions and tasks people must be able to do to get there (tiers 5 and 6). Each behaviour must be something a person could see or count at work, such as "confirms the fix with the customer before closing the complaint". Rewrite anything phrased as "understands", "is aware of" or "feels confident".
4. Choose measures tier by tier, favouring tier 5 and above:
   - **Tiers 1 to 3** (attendance, activity, learner perceptions): one line each at most. They help run the programme. LTEM treats them as weak evidence that anyone learned anything, so the plan never presents them as proof.
   - **Tier 4** (knowledge): only for facts people must recall with no reference to hand, such as a safety rule.
   - **Tier 5** (decision-making competence): realistic scenario questions where people choose what to do and why.
   - **Tier 6** (task competence): people do the task in a realistic practice or simulation, checked against a written standard.
   - **Tier 7** (transfer): evidence from the work itself: work records, system data, quality checks, audits, or a manager's observation using the Observation Guide skill. Survey answers about use are self-report; log them as supporting evidence and label them so.
   - **Tier 8** (effects of transfer): the team or business measures the behaviours should move.
   A tier 5 or 6 measure repeated some weeks after the course tells you more about what stuck than one taken on the day.
5. Give every measure a baseline: the same measure, for the same group, with the same definition, over a comparable period before the course. Where none exists, take one before the first session, or use a comparison group: a rollout in waves gives you trained and not-yet-trained teams to compare over the same weeks. If neither is possible, write "no baseline" in the plan and mark that measure's evidence as weaker.
6. For every tier 8 measure, name the tier 7 behaviour expected to move it, how long that should take to show, and the other things that could move the same number in the same period (staffing, a new system, a process or contract change, the season). Plan to log those as they happen, so the Learning Impact Report can show the programme's contribution honestly.
7. Set the timetable with real dates: baselines before the course; tiers 4 to 6 at the end of the course and again at [30 days]; the Application Survey at about day 60; tier 7 at [60 to 90 days]; tier 8 at [six months]. For a system or process, count from the go-live date if people can't use it before then. Name an owner by role for each measure.
8. Fill the Kirkpatrick column with this crosswalk: Level 1 Reaction is LTEM tier 3; Level 2 Learning covers tiers 4 to 6; Level 3 Behaviour is tier 7; Level 4 Results is tier 8. Tiers 1 and 2 have no Kirkpatrick level.
9. Write the data notes: results reported in aggregate for groups of [10] or more, who sees what, how long data is kept, and that only grouped or anonymised data goes into any AI tool. For safety-critical or regulated training, state that the plan evaluates the programme and that competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under the organisation's procedure.
10. Keep to [six to eight] measures the team has time to collect. A small L&D team cuts tier 1 to 3 measures first and never cuts the only tier 7 measure.
11. Run the Quality checks below and fix what fails before showing anything.
12. Present the plan, then a short **Assumptions** list and a **Check before you use it** list: that each source exists and holds data for the baseline period, that the owners have agreed, and that the dates fit the course timetable.
13. Offer a .docx, or the next skill: Application Survey for the day-60 survey, Success Case Interviews to find out why some people used it and others didn't, or Learning Impact Report once the data is in.

## Quality checks

- The plan has at least one measure at LTEM tier 5 or 6 and at least one at tier 7, and no attendance, completion or satisfaction measure is labelled as learning, behaviour or results.
- Every measure has a baseline with a value and a period, or a named comparison group, or is marked "no baseline" with its evidence called weaker.
- Every measure has a named source, an owner by role and a date.
- Every tier 8 result names the tier 7 behaviour that should move it and at least one other factor that could move the same number.
- Every behaviour is something a person could see or count at work; none uses "understand", "be aware of" or "feel confident".
- Every measure is reported for groups of [10] or more, and nothing in the plan tracks, ranks or signs off a named learner.
- The Kirkpatrick column follows the crosswalk in Step 8.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: Claude drafts the plans, nudges and reports. Managers do the coaching, and a person makes every call about someone's skill. This plan measures a programme. No measure in it judges, ranks or signs off an individual. For safety-critical or regulated training, competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under your organisation's procedure, whatever the evaluation shows.
- Personal data: quiz scores, survey answers and observation notes about a named person are personal data under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Collect only what the evaluation needs, report it in aggregate, and group or anonymise it before it goes near any AI tool. Follow the ICO's guidance and your organisation's AI policy.
- Fairness: if results differ between groups (sites, shifts, part-time staff), look first at differences in opportunity, such as time, rotas, tools and manager support, before drawing any conclusion about the people. Breakdowns by protected characteristic come only from your organisation's equality monitoring, in aggregate and above the threshold. Never infer a characteristic.
- Equality Act 2010: scenario questions, practical tasks and observations need reasonable adjustments for disabled learners, and every material must be accessible. Check the current law and guidance; this plan is a draft, never legal advice.
- No invented facts: baselines, targets, dates and sources come from the user or stay as placeholders.
