# Manager Pre-Course Brief

Produces two things for one programme: a short email from L&D to each learner's line manager, and a 15-minute conversation guide the manager uses with each learner before the course. Or checks a brief someone has drafted. It gets the manager involved at the point that counts most: in Broad and Newstrom's transfer matrix, what the learner's manager does before the training ranked as the most powerful point for transfer.

## When to use

- "Lead the Shift starts in three weeks. What should managers say to their people beforehand?"
- "Write the email to line managers before the new system training."
- "Give our team leaders a short script for a chat before the complaints course."
- "Check this manager briefing before it goes out."
- "Managers send people on courses and never mention them. How do I get them talking before day one?"

Wrong tool for what the manager does after the course (use Manager Follow-Up Guide) or for the learner's joining instructions (use Course Joining Note).

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** the programme: its name, what it covers, the dates, and who attends by role. [one cohort, with managers briefed at least two weeks before day one]
2. **Essential:** the one to three behaviours the programme should change at work, ideally from On-the-Job Objectives or a Performance Gap Brief. [drafted from the course outline and marked as proposed]
3. The business measure the programme serves. [placeholder]
4. The support managers can realistically give: protected time, a first chance to use the skill, cover. [30 minutes a week of protected time for the first month, marked as proposed]
5. When the review happens. [20 minutes within two weeks of the course, booked during the pre-course conversation]

Never ask for learners' names, performance ratings, absence records or health details. The guide works for every learner in the cohort, and the manager brings what's personal to the conversation. If the user includes any of this, leave it out and say so.

## Steps

Numbers in square brackets in these steps and the Quality checks are defaults. Use the user's own figure if they give one; otherwise use the default without asking.

1. Read what the user has given, including any course outline, objectives or earlier briefing. If they've given an existing brief, email or conversation guide, this is a check job: run the Quality checks on it, list every problem with where it sits and why it matters, then write a corrected version.
2. Ask for missing essentials in one message, five questions at most, with the defaults shown. If they say "just draft it", use the defaults and list them as assumptions.
3. Fit the brief to the programme. Decide whether the course builds an open skill, used in judgement calls with people (leading a team, handling a complaint, a client conversation), or a closed one with a set right way (a system, a procedure, a safety check). For open skills the things to try are situations ("your next one-to-one"). For closed skills they're the first real tasks ("the first five jobs you close in the new app").
4. Choose the things to try: one or two behaviours from the input list, never the whole course outline. Write each as something the manager could see or hear at work, with the situation and the standard, the way Mager-style performance objectives set out behaviour, condition and standard. Then turn each into an if-then plan, the implementation intention format from Gollwitzer's research: "If [situation at work], then I will [behaviour]." The learner rewrites it in their own words during the conversation.
5. Write the email to the manager, under 200 words, in this order: the programme and its dates; one sentence on why their part matters, citing only the Broad and Newstrom manager-before rank; the ask (a 15-minute conversation with each learner before [date], using the guide); what comes after the course (a follow-up guide from L&D); a reply-by date so L&D knows the conversations happened. Report the replies as a count per team.
6. Write the conversation guide in four parts, each with a time, a few sentences the manager can say, and two or three open questions:
   - **Why you, why now** (4 min): what the programme is for and why the manager has put this person forward, in terms of their role and what's coming (a new team, a new system, a new contract). Present the course as an investment in the person. Rewrite any line that presents it as a punishment, a warning, a last chance or a fix for a performance problem. A real performance concern belongs in a separate conversation under the organisation's performance procedure, and the course stays out of it.
   - **What to try afterwards** (5 min): the one or two if-then plans. The learner picks or reshapes them, and together they name the first real situation, within [5] working days of the course.
   - **Support and time** (3 min): what the manager will do, with an amount or a date: protected time, the first opportunity, cover, a lighter workload in the first week back. Ask "What could get in the way?" and agree one fix.
   - **Review date** (3 min): book the review there and then, within [two] weeks of the course. Say it's a conversation about what they tried and what got in the way, kept apart from appraisal ratings and pay.
7. Write an **Avoid saying** box with lines to steer clear of and what to say instead: lines that turn the course into a sanction ("this should sort your attendance out"), lines that tie it to a rating, pay or keeping the job, and promises the manager can't keep.
8. Add one access line to the guide: the manager asks whether anything would make the course easier to take part in, and passes the request (never the reason) to L&D.
9. Check the guide's times add up to 15 minutes and the conversation is dated before day one.
10. Run the Quality checks below and fix what fails. Mark any value you supplied (a support offer, a review window) as proposed.
11. Present the email and the guide, then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**: the dates, that the things to try match what the course teaches, that managers can give the support offered, and that review dates fit rotas and shifts.
12. Offer a .docx, a Teams-length version of the email, a one-page card the manager can take into the conversation, or the Course Joining Note and Sponsor Message skills for the rest of the pre-course set.

## Output format

**1. Email to managers**
Subject: [Programme], [dates]: a 15-minute conversation before it starts
Under 200 words, in the order set out in step 5. Signed by [L&D role].

**2. Conversation guide: [programme], before [date]**
For [manager role] and [learner role]. 15 minutes, in work time, somewhere private.

| Part | Time | Say | Ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Why you, why now | 4 min | [two or three sentences] | [open questions] |
| What to try afterwards | 5 min | | |
| Support and time | 3 min | | |
| Review date | 3 min | | |

**What we agreed** (filled in together, kept by the learner and the manager)
- I'll try: If [situation], then I will [behaviour].
- First chance: [situation], by [date].
- My manager will: [support], [amount or date].
- Review: [date], [time], [place].

**Avoid saying**

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| [risky line] | [line to use] |

Email and guide together fit on two pages.

## Quality checks

- Every line presents the course as preparation or investment, and none presents it as a punishment, a warning, a last chance or a fix for a performance problem.
- The guide names one or two things to try, each an observable behaviour in a named work situation with an if-then plan, and lists no course topics in their place.
- The manager commits to at least one specific piece of support with an amount or a date.
- A review date within [two] weeks of the course is booked in the conversation, and the review is kept apart from ratings, pay and performance procedures.
- The guide's times add up to 15 minutes, and the conversation takes place before day one.
- The email is under 200 words and cites only the manager-before rank from Broad and Newstrom.
- No learner names, ratings, absence records or health details appear anywhere.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: Claude drafts the plans, nudges and reports. Managers do the coaching, and a person makes every call about someone's skill. Claude drafts the email and the guide. The manager holds the conversation and agrees with the learner what to try, and nothing in the brief assesses the learner.
- Personal data: the guide uses roles, never names. If L&D tracks which conversations happened, report it as a count per team. Under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, notes about a named learner are personal data; follow the ICO's guidance and your organisation's AI policy before pasting any in.
- Fairness: every learner in the cohort gets the same guide, and who goes on the course is decided on the same criteria for everyone. Never infer or mention protected characteristics, and check the wording for assumptions about who needs the course or what they can manage.
- Law: the Equality Act 2010 covers reasonable adjustments for disabled learners on training. A genuine performance concern follows your own procedure and Acas guidance. For safety-critical or regulated training, the course alone makes nobody competent; sign-off stays with a qualified person under your procedure. Check the current versions. This is a draft, never legal advice.
- No invented facts: dates, measures, support offers and quotes come from the user or stay as placeholders.
