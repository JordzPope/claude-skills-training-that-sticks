# Practice Challenges

Drafts four weekly challenges for the first month after a course, each asking people to use the skill once on real work, plus a short note telling managers what each week asks. It gives L&D something concrete to send in weeks 1 to 4, while the course is fresh and before old habits settle back in.

## When to use

- "Give me four weekly challenges for advisers after our customer course."
- "What can new supervisors practise in their first month back?"
- "Turn the trainer's notes into practice tasks for after the course."
- "We want a 30-day challenge for the new app rollout."

Drafting only. Bring rough notes, a trainer's ideas or last cohort's challenges as input, and the skill lists the problems in them as it drafts. Wrong tool for the dated follow-up schedule (Spaced Follow-Up Plan), the messages that send each challenge (Reinforcement Nudges) or practice inside the course room (Practice Scenarios).

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** the programme and the two to four things people should do differently at work.
2. **Essential:** the real work learners do in a normal week where those things come up: the tasks, how often, and which parts sit outside their role or control.
3. The course end date and the first challenge week. [four weeks, starting the Monday after the course]
4. Targets and time: what learners are measured on (calls handled, handle time, jobs per day) and how much extra time managers have agreed. [up to 15 minutes of extra time a week, still to be agreed with managers]
5. How people share what happened: a one-to-one, the team huddle, a peer group or a Teams channel. [a two-line message to their manager]
6. Any rough notes, trainer's ideas or last cohort's challenges. [none]

Never ask for learners' names, results or health details. If someone needs an adjustment, ask only what the challenge must allow (more time, another format), never why.

## Steps

1. Read what the user has given. This skill drafts only: if they've given rough notes or an existing set of challenges, list every problem in them first, with where it sits and why it matters, then draft with each problem fixed or left as a visible placeholder.
2. Ask for missing essentials in one message, five questions at most, showing the defaults. If they say "just draft it", use the defaults, leave placeholders and list the assumptions.
3. Map each behaviour to where it shows up in the learners' real week: the task, what sets it off, and how often. Build challenges only on work every learner in the group gets. If some won't meet the situation in time (another queue, another shift, a quiet patch), give them a second route: shadow a colleague, review a recorded example with their manager, or move the challenge to a later week.
4. Sequence the four weeks from small to full. Week 1: one small step inside a routine task. Week 2: the whole method on a routine task. Week 3: a harder case or a variation they'll meet. Week 4: use it unprompted, then tell a colleague what they now do differently.
5. Fit the challenges to the kind of skill. Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang's 2010 meta-analysis found transfer links to a supportive work environment, among other things, and that most predictors are stronger for open skills such as leadership and customer handling than for closed ones such as a software procedure. For an open skill, build the manager or a peer into more of the weeks. For a closed skill, aim the challenges at the steps people skip or get wrong on the live system.
6. Write each challenge to the same shape:
   - a short, plain name;
   - the one action and its moment as an if-then plan, using Gollwitzer's implementation intentions: "When [situation at work], I will [action]", leaving the learner room to name their own moment;
   - the extra time it takes across the week, 15 minutes or less;
   - what done looks like: something they did in their work that they or their manager could see;
   - one or two reflection questions: what happened, and what would you change;
   - how to share it, with whom and by when;
   - an easier route and a stretch route.
7. Check every challenge against the job. The action sits within the learner's role and control, so an outcome they can't control (a customer's reaction, a client agreeing) never counts as done. Where a challenge would touch a target such as handle time or jobs per day, estimate the extra time, write the trade-off into the manager note and mark the challenge "agree with managers before sending". Every challenge happens on real work; quizzes, essays and reflective write-ups belong elsewhere.
8. Keep it encouraging and fair. No leaderboard, points per person, prizes for the most completions or public lists of names; completion is shared only as a cohort count. The easier route lets anyone who needs a reasonable adjustment take part, for example a written option in place of speaking at a huddle, or more time.
9. For safety-critical or regulated tasks, challenges practise inside the organisation's procedure, with the supervision it requires. Competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under the procedure, and finishing a challenge never counts towards it.
10. Add a short manager note: what each week asks, the time it needs and any trade-off to agree, and one question to ask at the next one-to-one or huddle. Managers do the coaching; the note gives them the prompt.
11. Run the Quality checks and fix what fails.
12. Present the four challenges and the manager note, then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**: every learner meets the situation in each week, managers have agreed the time against targets, and the share route works for shift and field staff.
13. Offer a .docx, the messages that send each challenge (Reinforcement Nudges), or a Peer Practice Circle where people compare what they tried.

## Output format

**Practice challenges: [programme]** · weeks of [dates] · for [roles] · prepared by [role]

For each week:

**Week [n]: [challenge name]** · [date range]
- **Your challenge:** When [situation at work], I will [action].
- **Time:** [extra minutes across the week, 15 at most]
- **Done looks like:** [what they did, visible in their work]
- **Easier route:** [option] · **Stretch:** [option]
- **Afterwards:** [one or two reflection questions]
- **Share it:** [with whom, how, by when]

**Note for managers** (half a page): what each week asks; time needed and any target trade-off, with [agreed by: role, date]; one question per week for the one-to-one or huddle; completion reported as a cohort count only.

One page for learners, half a page for managers. Then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**.

## Quality checks

- Every challenge happens on a real task from the learners' normal week, and its one action is within their role and control.
- Every learner in the group meets the situation in that week, or the challenge gives a second route.
- Each challenge adds 15 minutes or less across the week, and any that touches a target is marked for the manager's agreement in the manager note.
- The four weeks build from one small step on routine work to the full skill on harder cases.
- Each challenge has an if-then line, what done looks like, a reflection question, a share route and an easier route.
- Nothing ranks, scores or names individual learners: no leaderboard, points or public lists, and completion appears only as a cohort count.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: Claude drafts the plans, nudges and reports. Managers do the coaching, and a person makes every call about someone's skill. Challenges give people practice and give managers something to coach on; finishing one is never a judgement of anyone's ability.
- Personal data: work with roles. Who completed which challenge, and what they wrote about it, is personal data under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, so share it as a cohort count, follow the ICO's guidance and your organisation's AI policy, and never paste named reflections into Claude.
- Fairness and access: the same challenges for everyone, with a second route for anyone who won't meet the situation. Under the Equality Act 2010, reasonable adjustments apply to practice activities, so every challenge has an easier route and an accessible format on request, and nobody has to explain why they need one.
- Safety-critical or regulated tasks: practice happens inside the organisation's procedure and supervision, and competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under that procedure.
- Check the current versions of the law and guidance named here. This is a draft, never legal advice.
- No invented facts: tasks, targets, dates and time agreements come from the user or stay as placeholders.
