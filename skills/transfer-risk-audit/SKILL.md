# Transfer Risk Audit

Checks a planned programme against what predicts whether people use what they learn back at work, and names the weak spots with a fix and an owner for each. Or checks an audit or transfer plan someone has already written. It looks at the programme and the workplace around it, so an L&D team can close the gaps before launch, while there's still time to brief managers and change the design.

## When to use

- "We launch the new supervisors' programme in January. Will it stick?"
- "Audit this provider's proposal for learning transfer before we sign."
- "Our annual compliance e-learning gets 100% completion and nothing changes. What's missing?"
- "How do we get managers involved before the course starts?"
- "Check the transfer section of this programme plan."

Wrong tool for judging whether any learner has used the training or is competent, which is a call for their manager or a qualified person. Once a programme is running and people report blockers, Barrier Removal Plan goes deeper on what stops them at work.

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** which programme, and the plan for it: audience, objectives, format, length, dates, and anything planned before, during and after the course (a plan, a provider's proposal or a few lines).
2. **Essential:** the work it applies to: where and when people will use it, and what gets in the way today. [unknown; Claude marks every work-environment finding as an assumption to confirm]
3. What managers are asked to do, if anything. [nothing planned]
4. What follow-up exists after the course: job aids, refreshers, check-ins, spaced practice. [none]
5. Whether the content is safety-critical or regulated. [no]

Never ask for learner names, quiz scores or completion records by person, or anything else about individual learners. The audit needs the plan and the facts about the work.

## Steps

1. Read what the user has given, including attachments. If they've given an existing transfer audit, a transfer checklist or the transfer section of a plan or proposal, this is a check job: run the Quality checks on it, list each problem with where it sits and why it matters, then write a corrected audit. If they've given a programme plan, draft the audit from it.
2. Ask for missing essentials in one message, five questions at most, with the defaults shown. If they say "just draft it", use the defaults and list them as assumptions.
3. Summarise the programme in a few lines and say whether the skill is **open** (judgement and people skills, such as leading a team or handling a complaint) or **closed** (a fixed procedure, such as logging a job in an app). Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang's 2010 meta-analysis of 89 studies found most predictors of transfer stronger for open skills, so for an open skill give the work environment and motivation more weight.
4. Assess Baldwin and Ford's three groups of factors. Their 1988 review treats transfer as people applying what they learned across their work and keeping it up over time.
   - **Learner factors**, for the group as a whole: do people know why this matters to their own job, do they believe they can do it, and do they arrive with what the course assumes? Look at what the programme does to build that. Research also links personal traits to transfer; leave traits out of the audit, because the programme can't change them.
   - **Training design**: clear on-the-job objectives; content about people's real tasks; active practice close to the job, with feedback; recall and practice spaced over the weeks after, with gaps that grow with the time until people need the skill (Kim and colleagues found this pattern in a 2019 field study of 10,514 people in workplace training); and a plan from each learner for when and where they'll first use it. If-then plans ("if a customer rings back about the same fault, then I'll...") are Gollwitzer's implementation intentions, which raise the odds people act. Flag any design that matches teaching to learning styles: the evidence doesn't support it.
   - **Work environment**: a chance to use it soon after the course; time in the job; tools and information where the work happens; targets, bonuses or habits that reward the old way; support from the manager and peers; feedback on how it's going.
5. Fill Broad and Newstrom's transfer matrix: three roles (manager, learner, trainer or L&D) by three times (before, during and after the course). In each of the nine cells write what the plan has, quoting it, or "nothing planned". In their research the learner's manager acting before the training ranked as the most powerful point and the trainer before the training second, so rate an empty manager-before or trainer-before cell high risk whatever else is planned. What good looks like in each row:
   - Manager: before, explains why the person is going and agrees one or two things to try; during, protects the time; after, asks what they tried, gives them chances to use it and gives feedback.
   - Trainer or L&D: before, briefs managers and gathers real tasks; during, runs practice on those tasks and action planning; after, sends spaced follow-up and job aids and supports managers.
   - Learner: before, brings a real task and agrees goals with their manager; during, practises and writes their plan; after, uses it and reviews it with their manager or peers.
6. List the weak spots. For each give the factor or matrix cell, the evidence in the plan (quote the section) or "not in the plan", and a risk level: **high** (likely to stop most people using it at work), **medium** (will slow or weaken use) or **low**. Rate the programme and the workplace only.
7. Propose a fix for every high and medium risk: a specific action, an owner (a role) and a timing (before, during or after, with a date or placeholder). Fix the manager-before and trainer-before cells first. Name the pack skill that drafts each fix: Manager Pre-Course Brief, Real Work Brief, Transfer Action Plan, Spaced Follow-Up Plan, Reinforcement Nudges, Job Aid, Manager Follow-Up Guide or Barrier Removal Plan.
8. Check what the plan counts as evidence. Completion, attendance and quiz scores show only that people finished the course and recalled something on the day. For safety-critical or regulated training, any decision that someone is competent stays with a qualified person under the organisation's procedure. Flag a plan that treats a pass mark or a trainer's signature as sign-off.
9. Check access: accessible materials, and reasonable adjustments planned for the course, the practice and any assessment.
10. Run the Quality checks and fix what fails.
11. Present the audit, starting with **Fix first** (the two or three changes that remove the most risk), then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**: every finding marked as an assumption, each proposed owner and date, and anything about the work you couldn't confirm.
12. Offer a .docx, the pack skill for the first fix, or Weinbauer-Heidel's *What Makes Training Really Work* as further reading: a practitioner's synthesis of 12 levers of transfer across the trainee, the design and the organisation.

## Quality checks

- All three Baldwin and Ford factors are assessed, each with at least one finding tied to the plan or marked as an assumption.
- All nine matrix cells say what's planned or "nothing planned", and an empty manager-before or trainer-before cell is rated high risk.
- The audit rates the programme and the workplace only: no learner is named, scored or ranked, and no group is described by age or any other protected characteristic.
- Every weak spot cites where its evidence sits in the plan, or says "not in the plan".
- Every high or medium risk has a fix with an owner role and a timing; proposed owners and dates are marked and listed under Check before you use it.
- No finding or fix treats completion, attendance or a quiz score as evidence of use at work or of competence.
- Accessible materials and reasonable adjustments are checked for the course, the practice and any assessment.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: "Claude drafts the plans, nudges and reports. Managers do the coaching, and a person makes every call about someone's skill." The audit rates a programme and the workplace around it. Managers do the coaching its fixes call for, and a person decides whether anyone has used the training or is competent: their manager, or a qualified person under your procedure for safety-critical or regulated work.
- Personal data: the audit needs the plan and the facts about the work. Completion records, quiz scores and observation notes about named people are personal data under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018; aggregate any you share, and follow the ICO's guidance and your organisation's AI policy.
- Fairness: ask the same questions of every group in the audience, and never infer or mention protected characteristics, age included. Under the Equality Act 2010, reasonable adjustments apply to the course, the practice and any assessment, and materials should be accessible.
- Safety and regulated work: the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, the regulations for the topic and HSE guidance set what safety training must cover, and your organisation's procedure says who signs off competence.
- Law: check the current versions of the laws and guidance named here. This is a draft, never legal advice.
- No invented facts: what's planned, dates, owners and figures come from the user or stay as placeholders. Quote no single rate for how much training transfers; no reliable one exists.
