# Spaced Follow-Up Plan

Produces the dated schedule for the weeks after a course: when people recall what they learned, when they use it on real work, and when their manager checks in, with a channel and an owner for every touchpoint. Or checks a plan someone has already written. It turns "we'll send a follow-up email" into 8 to 12 weeks of follow-up an L&D team can actually run.

## When to use

- "Forty team leaders finish our management course next month. What should happen over the following 12 weeks?"
- "Build a reinforcement schedule for the new job-management app rollout."
- "Our follow-up is a thank-you email with the slides. What should it be?"
- "How far apart should the refreshers be for the annual safety course?"
- "Check this follow-up plan before I send it to the operations director."

Wrong tool for writing the messages (use Reinforcement Nudges), the recall questions (Retrieval Practice Set), the weekly tasks (Practice Challenges) or the manager's questions at each check-in (Manager Follow-Up Guide). This plan sets what happens when and who owns it; those skills write the content.

## Inputs

Ask once for anything missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Essential:** the programme: what it covers, who attends (roles and how many) and the date the course ends.
2. **Essential:** the two to four things people should do differently at work afterwards, and how often each one comes up in their job: daily, weekly, monthly or a few times a year. [from the user's objectives; if there are none, Claude proposes them from the course content and marks them as proposed]
3. Who the learners' line managers are, and the meetings they already hold with them: one-to-ones, huddles, toolbox talks. [a monthly one-to-one]
4. Where learners work and what they can see there: desk or field, email, Teams, text to a work phone, the LMS, a team huddle. [desk-based; email and Teams]
5. Plan length and who in L&D can own touchpoints. [10 weeks from the course end; the L&D coordinator sends, line managers hold the check-ins]
6. Dates to work around (peak periods, rota changes, bank holidays) and whether any materials need an accessible format. [UK bank holidays only; no format needs flagged]

Never ask for learners' names, scores, attendance by person or health details. The plan works at cohort level. If the user gives names, work with roles and say you've done so.

## Steps

1. Read what the user has given, including any attached course outline, objectives or plan. If they've given an existing follow-up plan, this is a check job: run the Quality checks on it, list each problem with where it sits and why it matters, then write a corrected plan.
2. Ask for missing essentials in one message, five questions at most, showing the defaults. If they say "just draft it", use the defaults, leave placeholders and list the assumptions.
3. Set a retention interval for each behaviour: how long until people next need it for real, and how long it has to last. A step used every day in a live system needs little spacing once it's in use. A conversation held twice a quarter, or a safety step needed on a rare day, needs recall spread well past the course. Apply Thalheimer's rule of thumb: make the spacing interval roughly match the retention interval. The longer people must hold on to it, the longer the gaps: something needed again within a week suits gaps of a few days, while something needed in three months suits gaps that grow to several weeks, with the later touchpoints spread across those months.
4. Space the touchpoints with gaps that grow. Start close to the course and lengthen each gap; for a 10-week plan, days 2, 7, 14, 28, 49 and 70 is a sound default. The evidence: a field study of 10,514 people in real workplace training (Kim, Wong-Kee-You, Wiseheart and Rosenbaum, *Behavior Research Methods*, 2019) found the best gap between retrieval events grows with the time until people need the skill, the same pattern as lab studies. Put no more than two touchpoints in the first seven days, and aim for 6 to 10 in total.
5. Give each touchpoint one type, and mix the types across the plan:
   - **Recall:** people answer two or three questions from memory, or explain a step, before they see the answer. Re-sent slides, recordings and recap cards are resources; list them separately and never count them as recall.
   - **Practice on real work:** one named use of the skill on a live task that week, with a prompt to note what happened.
   - **Manager check-in:** 10 to 15 minutes inside a meeting the manager already holds, on what the learner tried and what got in the way. Schedule one in the first two weeks, one around weeks 4 to 6 and one at the end. In Broad and Newstrom's transfer matrix, the learner's manager before the course ranked as the most powerful point, so if no pre-course conversation happened, make the first check-in a short version of it: why this matters here and what to try first.
   - Optional, where they fit: a peer session (Peer Practice Circle) or a job aid sent just before the moment of use (Job Aid).
6. Fit the mix to the kind of programme. Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang's 2010 meta-analysis found transfer links to a supportive work environment, among other things, and that most predictors are stronger for open skills such as leadership and customer handling than for closed ones such as a software procedure. So for management and customer programmes, add practice and manager check-ins. For systems and procedures, once people use the system daily, aim the touchpoints at the steps they use rarely or get wrong (exceptions, month-end). For compliance and safety content that must be right on a rare day, carry recall past 12 weeks with touchpoints at about 3 and 6 months, or just before the next refresher.
7. Date every touchpoint from the course end: calendar date, day number, type, what happens in one line, channel, owner role and how long it takes the learner (about 5 minutes for recall, 15 for practice or a check-in). Move dates off bank holidays, known peaks and rota changes; when a date moves, keep each gap at least as long as the one before.
8. Choose channels people can see at work. Desk staff: Teams or email. Field and shift staff: Teams or text on a work phone, or the team huddle or toolbox talk, sent at shift start and never needing a laptop. Anything that needs a desk or the LMS happens in paid time, with access arranged.
9. Name an owner for every touchpoint by role: L&D sends the recall and practice prompts; line managers hold the check-ins. CIPD's Learning at Work 2023 found perceptions of line manager support for transfer are generally low, so add a manager briefing, sent before the course ends, with each manager's check-in dates and what to ask.
10. Add a light check that the plan is running, in aggregate only: the share of people who answered each recall touchpoint, how many check-ins managers confirm they held, and what people say got in the way. Report by cohort or team, for groups of five or more.
11. For safety-critical or regulated content, the plan supports recall and practice. Competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under the organisation's procedure, so point to that procedure and never schedule a touchpoint that signs anyone off.
12. Run the Quality checks and fix what fails.
13. Present the plan, then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**: dates against rotas, peaks and holidays; managers' availability on their check-in dates; whether field staff can open each channel on a work phone; whether your LMS or Teams can schedule the sends.
14. Offer a .docx, the message for each touchpoint (Reinforcement Nudges), the recall questions (Retrieval Practice Set) or the managers' check-in questions (Manager Follow-Up Guide).

## Output format

**Spaced follow-up plan: [programme], [cohort]** · course ends [date] · plan runs to [date] · prepared by [role]

1. **What should stick**
   | Behaviour at work | How often it comes up | Retention interval | What the plan does about it |
2. **Schedule**
   | # | Date | Day | Type | What happens | Channel | Owner | Learner time |
   Type is Recall, Practice or Manager check-in (or Peer, Job aid). Proposed owners or dates are marked "(proposed)".
3. **Manager briefing:** sent [date] by [role]. Contents: why the course, what learners will try, each check-in date and what to ask.
4. **Resources available throughout:** slides, recording, job aid and where each lives. Kept out of the schedule.
5. **Is it running?** What's counted, from where, reviewed by [role] at weeks [n]. Aggregate only, groups of five or more.
6. **Access:** formats on offer and how a learner asks for an adjustment.

Then **Assumptions** and **Check before you use it**. One page plus the schedule table.

## Quality checks

- No more than two touchpoints fall in the first seven days after the course, and each gap between touchpoints is as long as or longer than the one before.
- The plan runs at least 8 weeks, and every behaviour used less than monthly has a recall or practice touchpoint in the last third of the plan.
- At least two touchpoints are recall, where people answer from memory, and at least two ask for practice on a named real-work task. Re-sent slides, recordings and recaps count as neither.
- Line managers own at least two check-ins, one of them in the first two weeks, and a manager briefing goes out before the course ends.
- Every touchpoint has a date, a channel the learners can see where they work, an owner role and a learner time; any date or owner Claude proposed is marked "(proposed)" and listed under Check before you use it.
- Results are reported for groups of five or more; no touchpoint names, scores or ranks an individual, or decides anything about someone's skill.

## Guardrails

- The one rule for this pack: Claude drafts the plans, nudges and reports. Managers do the coaching, and a person makes every call about someone's skill. This plan schedules the follow-up; the check-ins are the manager's conversations, and nothing in it judges whether a learner can do the job.
- Personal data: plan at cohort level and use roles. Quiz answers, LMS records and check-in notes about a named person are personal data under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, so aggregate or anonymise them before pasting anything into Claude, and follow the ICO's guidance and your organisation's AI policy. Never ask for health data, protected characteristics or personal contact details.
- Fairness and access: give everyone the same follow-up, shift and field staff included, and move the dates for anyone on leave. Under the Equality Act 2010, reasonable adjustments for disabled learners apply to follow-up activities as well as the course, and materials should be accessible.
- Safety-critical or regulated content: competence sign-off stays with a qualified person under the organisation's procedure, the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and any sector regulator's rules. No touchpoint stands in for it.
- Check the current versions of the law and guidance named here. This is a draft plan, never legal advice.
- No invented facts: dates, cohort sizes, meeting patterns and channels come from the user or stay as placeholders. Leave out forgetting-curve percentages and any single figure for how much training transfers; the research gives no reliable one.
